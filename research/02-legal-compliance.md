## 1. The core distinction: lottery vs sweepstakes vs skill contest

### The three-element test

Across US states, an **illegal lottery** is defined by the simultaneous presence of three
elements:

1. **Prize** — something of value awarded to the winner.
2. **Chance** — the winner is determined by randomness rather than skill.
3. **Consideration** — the entrant gives up something of value to enter.

If all three are present and the operator is not a state lottery (or otherwise licensed),
the promotion is an illegal lottery in most US states. Remove **any one** element and the
promotion becomes a lawful category:

| Elements present | Category | Legality |
|---|---|---|
| Prize + Chance + Consideration | **Lottery** | Illegal for private operators in nearly all states; reserved to state lotteries and licensed/charitable gaming |
| Prize + Chance (no consideration) | **Sweepstakes / giveaway / prize draw** | Generally lawful, subject to registration, rules and disclosure requirements |
| Prize + Consideration (no chance — winner picked on skill/merit) | **Skill contest** | Generally lawful, but several states restrict paid-entry skill contests, and "skill" must be genuine and judged on stated criteria |
| Prize alone (no chance, no consideration) | Gift / award | Not a prize promotion issue |

### What "consideration" actually means

Consideration is not limited to money. Practitioners split it two ways:

- **Monetary consideration** — buying a product, buying a ticket, paying an entry fee,
  a premium-rate SMS charge, or any payment that is a condition of entry. This is the
  clear-cut case.
- **Non-monetary consideration** — an entrant's expenditure of *substantial* time,
  effort or burden. Depending on the state and the facts, this can include: travelling
  to a location, attending a sales presentation, completing a lengthy or onerous task,
  mandatory app download, mandatory social-media engagement, or surrendering
  detailed personal information beyond what is needed to administer the draw.

The critical legal question for non-monetary consideration is whether the burden is
significant enough to operate as a **barrier to free entry**. Trivial effort (typing a
name and email, or writing a postcard) is generally not consideration; substantial
effort may be.

State law diverges sharply here. Commentators commonly group states as:

- **Monetary-only** states — only payment counts as consideration.
- **States that expressly recognise non-monetary consideration** — commonly listed as
  Delaware, Florida, Georgia, Iowa, Kansas, Michigan, New Jersey, Ohio, Rhode Island,
  South Carolina, Texas and Wisconsin.
- **"Neutral"** states — statute silent; courts decide case-by-case.

Because a national promotion reaches all 50 states, the practical compliance posture is
to design for the **strictest** interpretation: keep the free entry path genuinely free
and genuinely easy.

### Why private lotteries are illegal in most US states

Lotteries are treated as a state monopoly. Nearly every state constitution or criminal
code prohibits lotteries except those the state itself runs or expressly licenses
(charitable bingo/raffles, tribal gaming, licensed casinos). At the federal level a
lattice of statutes reinforces this — notably **18 U.S.C. §§ 1301–1304** (importing/
transporting lottery tickets, mailing lottery matter, broadcasting lottery information)
and the mail-fraud/wire-fraud statutes when promotions are deceptive. The consequence
for a product like FairDraw is that "let anyone sell raffle tickets and we'll pick the
winner" is the one design that lands squarely in criminal-law territory in most states.

Source: [18 U.S.C. Ch. 61 — Lotteries](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/part-I/chapter-61) (accessed 2026-08-16)

### Skill contests are not a free pass

Converting chance to skill removes the lottery problem only if the skill element is
real: entries must be judged against **objective, pre-disclosed criteria** by qualified
judges, ties must be broken on skill, and the task must be one where skill genuinely
determines the outcome. A "skill" element that is trivial, or a random tiebreaker at the
end, can collapse the contest back into a chance promotion. Several states (e.g. those
regulating paid-entry contests) also restrict charging entry fees even for skill
contests.

Source: [Enns & Archer — Sweepstakes and Skill Contests: The Basics](https://www.ennsandarcher.com/s_basics.html) (accessed 2026-08-16)

## 2. Raffles specifically

### Why a paid-ticket raffle is usually a lottery

A raffle is the textbook lottery: entrants **pay** for a ticket (consideration), a
**prize** is awarded, and the winner is drawn at **random** (chance). All three elements
are present. Raffles are therefore only lawful where a statute carves them out — and
those carve-outs are almost universally limited to **charitable, religious, educational,
fraternal, veterans' and similar nonprofit organizations**, usually with:

- a **permit/registration/licence** issued by a state (and sometimes county or
  municipal) gaming or charitable-gaming regulator;
- **caps** on prize value, ticket price, and/or number of raffles per year;
- restrictions on **who may sell** tickets (often members only, often 18+);
- restrictions on **use of proceeds** (must go to the charitable purpose);
- **reporting** obligations after the draw;
- in many states, an outright ban on **online ticket sales / online drawings**, or a
  requirement that tickets be sold in person within the state.

### What a for-profit business can and cannot do

Generally **cannot**:

- Sell raffle tickets or charge an entry fee for a random-draw prize. That is an
  unlicensed lottery in most states, regardless of how the proceeds are used.
- Run a "raffle for charity" as a for-profit promoter without the charity holding the
  permit and, in many states, without the promoter itself registering as a
  **professional fundraiser / commercial co-venturer**.
- Cure the problem by calling it a "donation" or "suggested contribution" while
  making payment functionally necessary to enter.

Generally **can**:

- Run a **sweepstakes**: free entry, random winner, no purchase necessary — subject to
  official rules, AMOE, registration/bonding where thresholds are met.
- Run a **skill contest** with an entry fee, where lawful, if skill genuinely
  determines the outcome and it is judged on disclosed criteria.
- Run a **charitable promotion** where the business donates and consumers do not pay to
  enter (with commercial co-venturer registration where required).
- Provide **software/tooling** to nonprofits that hold their own permits — but note
  that supplying the draw mechanism for an unlawful lottery can create exposure, and
  several states regulate raffle-software vendors and online raffle platforms directly.

### State-by-state variation

Raffle law is genuinely state-specific and not harmonised. Variation covers: whether
raffles are permitted at all; which organisation types qualify; permit fees; prize
caps; whether tickets may be sold online or across state lines; whether electronic
random-number-generator draws are allowed; and reporting. A few states (e.g. Alabama,
Hawaii, Utah) have historically been the most restrictive on charitable gaming; others
allow it broadly with a permit. Any national raffle product must gate by state.

Source: [Sweeppea — Sweepstakes and Contest Laws by State](https://www.sweeppeasweeps.com/sweepstakes-and-contest-rules-by-state.html) (accessed 2026-08-16)

### Maine specifically (the owner's state)

Maine regulates raffles under **17 M.R.S. Chapter 62 (Games of Chance)**, administered by
the **Gambling Control Unit**, Maine Department of Public Safety. The former §1837 was
**repealed** (PL 2017, c. 284, Pt. KKKKK, §24); raffles are now governed by
**17 M.R.S. §1837-A**, amended repeatedly since (PL 2017 c. 284; PL 2017 c. 365;
PL 2019 c. 129; PL 2021 c. 136; PL 2021 c. 636; PL 2023 c. 86).

Key provisions as published by the Maine Legislature (accessed 2026-08-16):

| Provision | Content |
|---|---|
| §1837-A(1) | General rule: "a person or organization may not conduct a raffle without registering with the Gambling Control Unit" unless otherwise specified |
| §1837-A(2) | **No registration required** where total prize value is **$2,500 or less** — this exemption is available to *any* person or organization |
| §1837-A(2-A) | **No registration required** for an **eligible organization** (as defined in §1832(2)) where total prize value is **$10,000 or less** |
| §1837-A(3) | Noncash prizes in the **$2,500–$10,000** band require registration; such prizes **may not be exchanged for cash** |
| §1837-A(4) | Registered eligible organizations may run raffles up to **$150,000 noncash**, or **$10,000 cash per winner** |
| §1837-A(5) | Raffle chances/tickets **may not be sold by a person under 18** (limited youth-activity exceptions) |
| §1837-A(7) | Internet raffles may not be advertised in a way that targets minors |

Practical read for a Maine-based operator: the small **$2,500 prize-value exemption is
not organisation-restricted**, which makes Maine unusually permissive at the very low
end — but it is an exemption from *registration*, not a licence to run a commercial
raffle business, and the surrounding Chapter 62 framework (eligible organisations, use
of proceeds, conduct of games of chance under §1835-A) still applies. Conducting
unlicensed games of chance is a **criminal offence** in Maine. Crucially, a Maine
exemption does nothing for entrants in the other 49 states.

## 3. AMOE — Alternative Method of Entry / "No Purchase Necessary"

### What AMOE is

An **Alternative Method of Entry (AMOE)** is a free entry route offered alongside a paid
or purchase-linked route. It is the standard device for removing the **consideration**
element and keeping a promotion a lawful sweepstakes rather than a lottery. Typical AMOE
mechanics: mail-in a 3×5 card, an online free-entry form, a text keyword with no premium
charge, or in-store request.

### What it must satisfy

**1. "No purchase necessary" disclosure.** The promotion must state prominently, in the
rules and in advertising, that no purchase is necessary and that **a purchase will not
increase your chances of winning**. Mail-based promotions in particular are expected to
carry this language.

**2. Equal dignity (disclosure parity).** The free entry method must be publicised with
**equal prominence** to the paid method — same placement, same visibility, not buried in
a footnote or behind extra clicks. If a customer can find the paid route but not the free
route, equal dignity fails.

**3. Equal chance (odds parity).** This is the substantive half of the rule: a free
entrant must have the **same statistical chance of winning** as a paid entrant. In
practice that means:

- Every entry, however obtained, carries the **same weight** in the draw.
- Free and paid entries go into the **same pool** for the **same prizes** — free entrants
  are not shunted to a lesser prize tier.
- Entry **caps** must be the same across methods (if paid entrants can enter 10 times,
  free entrants must be able to enter 10 times).
- Awarding "bonus entries" for purchase — e.g. 1 entry free vs. 5 entries for buying —
  **breaks equal dignity** and is a common failure mode.

**4. Not unduly burdensome.** The AMOE must not be obscure, slow, expensive or
disproportionately laborious relative to the paid route; excessive burden can itself be
treated as consideration.

### Direct software implication for FairDraw

> The draw engine must place **free entries and paid/purchase-linked entries in a single
> pool at identical per-entry weight**, draw from that single pool for all prize tiers,
> and enforce identical per-entrant entry caps across entry methods. Any "weighted
> entries" feature that multiplies odds for purchase is a compliance landmine and, if
> offered at all, should be restricted to skill contests / non-US contexts and blocked by
> default. The receipt/audit record should show the entry-method breakdown so equal
> weighting is externally verifiable.

## 4. US state registration and bonding thresholds

Three states are the classic registration/bonding trio. **Figures below verified
2026-08-16 — re-verify before relying on them.**

### New York — N.Y. Gen. Bus. Law § 369-e

| Item | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Trigger | Total announced prize value **greater than $5,000**, where NY residents may enter |
| Filing | Register with the **New York Department of State** |
| Deadline | **At least 30 days** before the promotion start date |
| Security | **Surety bond** (or equivalent trust) in an amount **equal to the total value of all prizes offered**, in favour of the People of the State of New York; surety may cancel only on 30 days' written notice to the Department |
| After | Winner list obligations; keep records |

| Item | Requirement | Cite |
|---|---|---|

| Filing | File rules with the **Florida Dept. of Agriculture & Consumer Services (FDACS)** — portal at ConsumerCompliance.FDACS.gov | § 849.094(3) |

### Rhode Island — R.I. Gen. Laws § 11-50-1

| Item | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Trigger | Total announced prize value **in excess of $500**, **and** the promotion is offered by/through a **retail establishment** in Rhode Island to promote its retail business |
| Filing | File a statement with the **Rhode Island Secretary of State** |
| Fee | **$150** filing fee |
| Bond | **No bond required** |

### Quick threshold table

| State | Threshold | Lead time | Bond | Fee |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New York | > $5,000 prize value | 30 days | Yes — = total prize value | filing fee applies |
| Florida | > $5,000 prize value | 7 days | Yes — trust or bond = total prize value (5-yr waiver) | $100 |
| Rhode Island | > $500, retail establishments only | before start | No | $150 |

**Practical design rule:** keep total announced prize value at or under **$5,000** and
avoid retail-linked promotions and you sidestep all three registration regimes. That
$5,000 line is the single most useful number to build a warning around.

## 5. Required official rules and disclosures

Official Rules are the contract between sponsor and entrant. In a dispute, a regulator
or court reads the Rules first. In several states specific content is **statutorily
mandated**, not merely best practice.

### The statutory backbone

- **39 U.S.C. § 3001(k)** (Deceptive Mail Prevention and Enforcement Act, 1999) governs
  sweepstakes mailings: requires a clear and conspicuous statement that no purchase is
  necessary, that a purchase will not improve chances of winning, plus sponsor name/address,
  odds, quantity/estimated retail value of each prize, and payment schedule for
  installment prizes. Source: [39 U.S.C. § 3001, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/39/3001) (accessed 2026-08-16).
- **Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17539.15** requires the no-purchase-or-payment-necessary
  message to appear in the official rules **in its own paragraph, in capital letters, in a
  contrasting typeface no smaller than the largest typeface used in the rules**, and
  provides that entries not accompanied by an order "shall not be subjected to any
  disability or disadvantage in the winner selection process." It also bars representing
  that a purchase-accompanied entry is more likely to win. Source:
  [Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17539.15 (Justia, 2025 code)](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-bpc/division-7/part-3/chapter-1/article-2/section-17539-15/) (accessed 2026-08-16).
- **N.Y. Gen. Bus. Law § 369-e** requires the rules to be filed/published and the odds
  disclosed for registrable promotions (see §4 above).
- **FTC Act § 5** (unfair or deceptive acts or practices) is the federal catch-all: all
  material terms must be **clear and conspicuous**, and the promotion must actually be run
  the way the rules say. Source: [FTC, "Advertising and Marketing" business guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/advertising-marketing) (accessed 2026-08-16).

### The standard checklist of required elements

Consolidated from the statutes above and mainstream promotions-law practice
([Sideman & Bancroft, "Contests and Sweepstakes 101"](https://www.sideman.com/contests-and-sweepstakes-101/);
[Olshan, "Sweepstakes Law Basics"](https://www.olshanlaw.com/sweepstakes-law-basics); both accessed 2026-08-16):

| # | Element | Notes / traps |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **"NO PURCHASE NECESSARY"** + "a purchase will not improve your chances of winning" | Must be conspicuous; CA dictates typography |
| 2 | **Sponsor identity** — full legal entity name + physical address | A platform that only runs the draw should be named as *administrator*, not sponsor |
| 3 | **Eligibility** — minimum age (usually 18+/19+ in AL & NE, 21+ for alcohol), residency (e.g. "legal residents of the 50 US states and DC"), exclusions for employees/immediate family/household | Exclude states you have not cleared |
| 4 | **Entry period** — exact start and end **date, time, and time zone** | Ambiguous close times are a common dispute source |
| 5 | **How to enter** — every method, incl. the free AMOE, spelled out step by step | AMOE instructions must be as findable as the paid path |
| 6 | **Entry limits** — per person / per day / per household, and how duplicates are handled | Must match what the software actually enforces |
| 7 | **Prize description, quantity, and ARV** (approximate retail value) | ARV drives the registration thresholds and the tax form |
| 8 | **Odds of winning** — "odds depend on the number of eligible entries received" for random draws; stated numerically for instant-win/predetermined games | |
| 9 | **Winner selection method** — random drawing vs judged criteria; who draws; when; the judging criteria and weights if a contest | This is where a provably-fair receipt belongs |
| 10 | **Winner notification and claim deadline** — how notified, how long to respond, forfeiture/alternate-winner rules | |
| 11 | **Affidavit / release requirements** — see §6 | |
| 12 | **Winner list availability** — how to request the list of winners (address or URL) and by when | Required for many registrable promotions |
| 13 | **Taxes** — winner is responsible; 1099 issued where required | |
| 14 | **"Void where prohibited"** + explicit state/territory exclusions | A catch-all, **not** a cure for an illegal structure |
| 15 | **Limitation of liability / dispute resolution / governing law** | |
| 16 | **Privacy** — what data is collected and how used; link to privacy policy | |
| 17 | **Platform disclaimer** — "This promotion is not sponsored, endorsed or administered by, or associated with, [Platform]" | Required by Meta, X, TikTok — see §7 |

### Two rules that trip people up

1. **"Void where prohibited" does not legalize an illegal promotion.** It excludes
   jurisdictions; it does not convert a lottery into a sweepstakes.
2. **Abbreviated rules are allowed but not a substitute.** Short-form rules in the ad
   creative ("NO PURCHASE NECESSARY. 18+. US only. Ends 9/30/26. See official rules at
   [URL].") must link to the full rules, and the two must not conflict.

| **IRS** | Records supporting information returns: generally **at least 4 years** (employment/withholding-style guidance; commonly applied to 1099 backup). Source: [IRS, "How long should I keep records?"](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/how-long-should-i-keep-records) (accessed 2026-08-16) |
| **Practice norm** | Retain the full entry pool, rules version, drawing evidence, winner correspondence and affidavits **4 years** (some counsel say 7, matching the longest state statute of limitations for consumer claims). Source: [Olshan, "Sweepstakes Law Basics"](https://www.olshanlaw.com/sweepstakes-law-basics) (accessed 2026-08-16) |

**What the retained record should contain** (this is the direct product spec):

1. The exact **Official Rules text** in force at the time, version-stamped.
2. The **complete entry list** as of the draw — every entry, its source (paid / AMOE /
   free), timestamp, and per-entry weight.
3. Any entries **disqualified before the draw**, with the reason and timestamp.
4. The **randomness evidence**: seed/commitment, algorithm, the verifiable result.
5. **Winner identity, notification date, claim date, prize delivered, delivery date.**
6. Signed **affidavit / release** documents.
7. The **winners list** as published or filed.

### Winner verification: affidavit of eligibility and publicity/liability release

Standard practice for prizes of meaningful value (commonly triggered at ARV ≥ $600, and
frequently notarized above ~$5,000): before the prize ships, the potential winner signs an
**Affidavit of Eligibility and Liability/Publicity Release** confirming name, age,
address, and that they meet the eligibility terms; releasing the sponsor from liability;
and — where permitted by law — granting the right to use their name, likeness and
statements for publicity without further compensation. The Rules must state the deadline
(typically 5–10 days) and that failure to return it forfeits the prize and permits
selection of an alternate winner. Sources:
[Sweeppea, "Affidavits of Eligibility & Publicity/Liability Releases"](https://www.sweeppeasweeps.com/official-rules-center/affidavits-of-eligibility-publicity-liability-releases-understanding-sweepstakes-rules-requirements/);
[National Sweepstakes Company, "Why You Need an Affidavit"](https://nationalsweepstakescompany.com/blog/why-you-need-an-affidavit-to-claim-sweepstakes-prizes/) (both accessed 2026-08-16).

> **Publicity-release caveat:** a few states (notably **Tennessee**, and in practice
> **New York** and **Florida** for registered promotions) restrict conditioning a prize on
> a publicity release, so rules usually say "except where prohibited."

### Tax reporting — the threshold moved in 2026

- **Prizes awarded on or before 31 Dec 2025:** Form **1099-MISC** required at **$600** or
  more in aggregate prize value from the same payer in a calendar year.
- **Prizes awarded after 31 Dec 2025:** the **One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA)** raises
  the Form 1099-MISC threshold for prizes and awards to **$2,000**, with **automatic
  inflation adjustments beginning in 2027**. Sponsors no longer need to collect an SSN/TIN
  (Form W-9) from winners of prizes valued at **$1,999 or less**. Aggregation still
  applies: several prizes from the same sponsor to the same winner in one calendar year
  totalling **$2,000+** trigger the form.
  Sources: [Venable, "New Prize Tax Rules Raise the 1099 Threshold for Sweepstakes and Contests" (Jan 2026)](https://www.allaboutadvertisinglaw.com/2026/01/new-prize-tax-rules-raise-the-1099-threshold-for-sweepstakes-and-contests.html);
  [Frankfurt Kurnit, "New Tax Bill Raises Form 1099-MISC Threshold"](https://advertisinglaw.fkks.com/post/102kyn8/new-tax-bill-raises-form-1099-misc-threshold-what-it-means-for-consumer-promotio) (accessed 2026-08-16).
- **Unchanged:** the winner owes tax on the fair market value of the prize whether or not a
  1099 is issued; the sponsor files the 1099-MISC copy to the winner by **31 January** and
  to the IRS by **28 February** (paper) following the award year. Some **state** thresholds
  remain lower than the federal one — check the winner's state.
- **Gambling winnings are a different form entirely** (Form W-2G, with withholding). If a
  promotion is structured as paid-entry gambling, the tax posture changes too — another
  reason the sweepstakes/lottery line matters.

## 7. Platform promotion rules

Platform rules are **contract terms, not law** — but they are the fastest way to lose an
account, and they are enforced automatically. Every major platform pushes legal
responsibility onto the promoter and demands a "platform is not a sponsor" disclaimer.

### Meta (Facebook / Instagram)

Primary source: [Meta, Pages, Groups and Events Policies — "Promotions"](https://www.facebook.com/policies/pages_groups_events/) (accessed 2026-08-16).

- Promotions on Meta surfaces are administered **at the promoter's own risk**; the Page
  admin is **fully responsible for the lawful operation** of the promotion — official
  rules, offer terms, eligibility, and compliance with applicable regulations.
- Promotion terms must include **a complete release of Facebook/Meta by each entrant** and
  an acknowledgement that the promotion is **"in no way sponsored, endorsed or administered
  by, or associated with, Facebook"** (Instagram equivalent for IG).
- **Prohibited:** using *personal timelines and friend connections* to administer a
  promotion. Concretely banned patterns: **"share on your Timeline to enter"**,
  **"share on your friend's Timeline for additional entries"**, and
  **"tag your friends in this post to enter."**
- You may not encourage people to **tag themselves in content in which they do not appear**.
- **Practical read for a giveaway tool:** commenting, following, and (on Instagram) tagging
  a genuine friend in a *comment* are widely used and generally tolerated; what is
  explicitly prohibited is **share-to-timeline** mechanics and **tagging-as-entry on the
  post itself**. Secondary explainer:
  [Woobox, "Instagram Giveaway Rules"](https://woobox.com/articles/instagram-giveaway-rules-and-compliance) (accessed 2026-08-16).

### X (Twitter)

Primary source: [X Help, "Our guidelines for running promotions"](https://help.x.com/en/rules-and-policies/x-contest-rules) (accessed 2026-08-16 — the page returned HTTP 403 to automated fetch, so the points below are drawn from secondary summaries of it).

- **Discourage creating multiple accounts.** Users who spin up many accounts to enter are
  liable to have **all** of their accounts suspended, and the promoter can be held
  responsible for encouraging it. Rules should state multi-account entries are ineligible.
- **Discourage posting the same content repeatedly** ("post this exact tweet 50 times to
  enter"). Duplicate/near-duplicate posting is spam under X's rules and can get entrants
  filtered or suspended. Ask for varied/creative replies instead.
- **Ask entrants to @mention the promoter** so entries are findable, and note that
  protected/private accounts cannot be seen.
- Follow applicable law, and include the customary "not sponsored, endorsed or administered
  by, or associated with, X" disclaimer.
  Source: [Gleam, "Twitter/X Giveaway Rules and Guidelines"](https://gleam.io/blog/x-giveaway-rules/) (accessed 2026-08-16).

### TikTok

Two distinct regimes.

**TikTok Shop Giveaway and Promotions Policy** — applies to sellers and creators promoting
via listings, LIVE, and short video. Primary source:
[TikTok Shop Academy, "Giveaway and Promotions Policy"](https://seller-us.tiktok.com/university/essay?knowledge_id=10017760) (accessed 2026-08-16):

- Giveaways must be **entirely free — no purchase required**.
- **Prize value must be below $500.**
- **Cash and gift cards are strictly prohibited as prizes.**
- Giveaways **may not be listed as products**; use the official **LIVE Giveaway** feature
  where available.
- **"No extra actions can be required by the customer beyond entering."**
- **Gamification elements are not allowed.** Short-video promotions must be registered
  through Seller Center; clear on-screen disclosure is required during LIVEs.
- Purchase-based incentives (BOGO) are allowed under the same **under-$500** ceiling but
  cannot be dressed up as standalone giveaways.

**Organic TikTok giveaways** (not Shop): guidance converges on the Meta shape — full
Official Rules (eligibility, dates, prize, winner selection, "no purchase necessary")
disclosed in the video/caption, plus a **"This promotion is in no way sponsored, endorsed,
administered by, or associated with TikTok"** disclaimer. Source:
[ViralSweep, "Understanding TikTok Giveaway Rules"](https://www.viralsweep.com/blog/tiktok-giveaway-rules) (accessed 2026-08-16).

### YouTube

Primary source: [YouTube Help, "YouTube's contest policies and guidelines"](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1620498?hl=en) (accessed 2026-08-16). The strictest of the majors:

- **"Your contest must be free of charge to enter"** — YouTube adds "don't forget to check
  your local lottery laws!" **There is no paid-entry option on YouTube at all**, AMOE or not.
- Official Rules must **link to the YouTube Community Guidelines**, state that
  non-compliant entries are disqualified, contain all federal/state/local disclosures, be
  consistent with the YouTube ToS, and include a **legally compliant privacy notice**.
- Rules must **clearly state YouTube is not a sponsor** and must **require viewers to
  release YouTube from liability**.
- The contest must be **conducted exactly as the Official Rules describe**.
- You **may not** ask viewers to transfer ownership or all rights in their entries to you.
- **Contests may not be run through ad units**; you may not associate YouTube with the
  contest without prior written consent.
- **No metric manipulation** — you and any third party may not manipulate YouTube metrics
  to misrepresent genuine viewer engagement.

### Twitch

Twitch publishes no standalone contest policy; giveaways fall under the **Terms of Service**
and **Community Guidelines**, plus Twitch's gambling-content policy. Operative constraints:

- The giveaway must not violate, or encourage violation of, the ToS/Community Guidelines —
  including giving away restricted or prohibited items.
- Twitch giveaways are normally structured as **sweepstakes: free entry, random draw**.
  Where any paid path exists, an **AMOE with equal odds** is required.
- Post Official Rules the moment a real prize is on the line.
- Source: [Gleam, "Twitch Giveaway Rules"](https://gleam.io/guides/twitch-giveaway-rules) (accessed 2026-08-16).

> **The bits/subs trap:** awarding extra entries for subscriptions, bits, or gifted subs is
> paid consideration. Without a free, equal-weight AMOE, that is a private lottery.

### Discord

Discord runs its own sweepstakes and publishes **Master Sweepstakes Rules** — a useful model
document. Primary source: [Discord Support, "Master Sweepstakes Rules"](https://support.discord.com/hc/en-us/articles/16000885744791-Master-Sweepstakes-Rules) (accessed 2026-08-16). It opens with the canonical formula: **"NO PURCHASE NECESSARY TO ENTER OR TO WIN. A PURCHASE OR PAYMENT WILL NOT IMPROVE YOUR CHANCES OF WINNING."**

For server-run giveaways there is no dedicated policy page, so the general ToS and Community
Guidelines apply; monetised or sponsored giveaways pull in Discord's **Monetization Policy**
(18+, two-factor authentication enabled). Source:
[Gleam, "Discord Giveaway Rules"](https://gleam.io/guides/discord-giveaway-rules) (accessed 2026-08-16).

### Cross-platform summary

| Platform | Paid entry allowed? | Platform disclaimer | Notable prohibition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meta / Instagram | Not addressed (law governs) | Yes + full release of Meta | Share-to-timeline; tag-friends-on-post as entry |
| X | Not addressed | Customary | Multi-account entries; repetitive identical posts |
| TikTok Shop | **No — free only** | Yes | Cash/gift-card prizes; prizes $500+; gamification |
| YouTube | **No — free only** | Yes + viewer release | Ad-unit contests; rights grabs; metric manipulation |
| Twitch | Governed by law; AMOE needed | Customary | Prohibited-item prizes; bits/subs-only entry |
| Discord | Governed by law | Customary | Monetized giveaways without 18+/2FA compliance |

**Engagement-bait note:** Meta's Feed and Instagram ranking systems demote "engagement bait"
(explicitly asking for likes/shares/tags/comments). That is a **distribution penalty**, not
a policy violation — but it means tag-a-friend mechanics can suppress the very post they are
meant to amplify.

## 8. Crypto / NFT raffles — why they attract extra scrutiny

Nothing about a blockchain changes the three-element test. A crypto raffle is analysed
exactly like any other raffle: prize + chance + consideration = lottery. What crypto adds is
**four extra regulators on top of gambling law**.

### Why the risk stacks

1. **Consideration is unusually easy to prove.** An on-chain payment is an immutable,
   timestamped, publicly auditable record that entrant X paid Y to enter. Prosecutors do not
   need discovery — the evidence is already published. This is the single most
   underappreciated feature of "provable fairness": the same ledger that proves you did not
   cheat also proves you took consideration.
2. **Securities law layers on top.** Where the raffle ticket, token or NFT carries any
   expectation of profit from others' efforts (revenue share, staking rewards, floor-price
   promises), state and federal securities regulators treat it as an unregistered security —
   independently of the gambling analysis.
3. **Money transmission / AML.** Taking value in and paying value out can implicate FinCEN
   money services business registration and state money transmitter licensing.
4. **"Sweepstakes casino" statutes now name software vendors.** See below — this is the
   direct threat to a tool, not just to its users.

### Notable enforcement

| Date | Action | Takeaway |
|---|---|---|
| **Oct 2022** | **Texas, Kentucky, New Jersey and Alabama** securities regulators issued cease-and-desist orders against **Slotie NFT**, a metaverse casino that sold 10,000 NFTs conferring ownership-like interests, staking rewards and a revenue split from casino games and **lotteries**. All four states found the NFTs were **unregistered securities**, and found fraudulent misstatements. 30 days to comply; exposure of 2–10 years' imprisonment plus fines. ### What this means concretely

- **Dual-currency mechanics are radioactive.** "Buy Gold Coins, get free Sweeps Coins that
  are redeemable for cash/crypto" is precisely the model California, New York and Maine
  criminalised. A promotions tool must not ship a purchasable-credit-plus-redeemable-entry
  currency, however it is labelled.
- **"Supporting" is enough.** AB 831 and the Stake.us complaint both reach service
  providers. A drawing tool that knowingly powers a paid-entry crypto raffle is not
  insulated by being "just software."
- **Token-gating is consideration if the token must be bought.** Requiring holdership of a
  token that entrants must purchase to enter is payment by another name. Free airdrops,
  pre-existing holdings and a genuine free AMOE are the mitigations — and the AMOE analysis
  in §3 applies unchanged. Source: [Lexology, "What is an NFT Sweepstakes AMOE?"](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=10d1ce08-8cbc-4e0b-901e-3589f67e6b59) (accessed 2026-08-16).
- **Gas fees are a grey area.** Requiring an on-chain transaction to enter imposes a real
  cost on the entrant even if the sponsor receives nothing. The conservative reading treats
  a mandatory gas-cost entry as consideration and provides an off-chain free AMOE.

## 9. Brief non-US note

### United Kingdom

Primary source: [Gambling Commission, "Free draws and prize competitions"](https://www.gamblingcommission.gov.uk/public-and-players/guide/page/free-draws-and-prize-competitions) (accessed 2026-08-16), under the **Gambling Act 2005**.

The UK draws the same three-way distinction as the US, with clearer official wording:

- **Lottery** — prizes allocated by chance with a required payment to enter. Regulated; a
  lottery **cannot be run for commercial or private gain**. This is the key structural
  difference from the US: in Britain, private commercial lotteries are not merely
  unlicensed, they are categorically unavailable — only licensed National Lottery and
  society/charitable lotteries may run.
- **Free draw** — all entries free, **or** entrants may choose between a paid route and a
  free route. Not gambling, **no licence needed**.
- **Prize competition** — outcome determined by the participant's **skill, judgement or
  knowledge**, and the requirement must be genuine enough to *"discourage people wishing to
  enter"* or *"prevent a proportion of those who enter from winning a prize."* Not gambling,
  no licence needed. Multiple-choice questions and "try again" formats rarely qualify.
  See also [ASA/CAP, "Promotional marketing: Free entry routes"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/promotional-marketing-free-entry-routes.html) (accessed 2026-08-16).

**The UK free-entry-route test, quoted** — this is the cleanest statement anywhere of what
FairDraw must implement:

1. *"the free entry route can be no more expensive and must be no less convenient than the
   paid route"*
2. *"the free entry route must be promoted and displayed at the same level as the paid for
   routes"*
3. *"the choice between the free entry route and the paid entry route must be promoted so
   that people who want to participate can see it"*
4. **"the system that determines prizes should not recognise the two routes as different"**

Point 4 is a **software requirement stated by a regulator**: the draw engine must be blind
to entry provenance.

### Canada

Two federal layers, and the reason every Canadian contest has a maths question:

- **Criminal Code** — lotteries and games of pure chance conducted for private gain are
  prohibited; provincial governments hold the monopoly. Removing **chance** (by adding a
  genuine skill element) is the standard route out.
- **Competition Act s. 74.06** — a promotional contest is reviewable conduct unless adequate
  and fair disclosure is made of the number and approximate value of prizes, the area(s) to
  which they relate, any fact materially affecting the chances of winning, that the winner
  is selected (or the prize distributed) **on the basis of skill or on a random basis**, and
  that **no undue delay** occurs in distributing prizes.
- **The skill-testing question (STQ)** is the practical consequence: potential winners must
  correctly answer, unaided and within a time limit, a question of genuine skill — by
  convention a **four-step arithmetic problem** (e.g. `(12 × 4) + 20 − 8 =`). It is asked of
  the *selected* entrant before the prize is confirmed, not of every entrant at entry.
  Sources: [Canadian Advertising/Marketing Lawyer, "Why Are Skill-Testing Questions Required
  For Canadian Contests?"](https://www.canadianadvertisinglaw.com/skilltestingquestioncanadiancontestlaw/);
  [ContestLawyer.ca, "Skill Testing Questions"](https://www.contestlawyer.ca/skill-testing-questions/) (both accessed 2026-08-16).
- **Quebec is no longer special.** Historically, any contest open to Quebec residents with
  total prize value over CA$100 had to be registered with the **Régie des alcools, des
  courses et des jeux**, with fees and document filing. **Bill 17, assented 27 October 2023,
  repealed the publicity-contest regime entirely** — no more Quebec registration or duties.
  French-language versions of rules and creative are still required for the Quebec market,
  and the Criminal Code and Competition Act still apply. Sources:
  [Cassels, "Let the Games Begin: Quebec Repeals Laws Related to the Regulation of Publicity Contests"](https://cassels.com/insights/let-the-games-begin-quebec-repeals-laws-related-to-the-regulation-of-publicity-contests/);
  [Miller Thomson, "Quebec puts an end to contest laws"](https://www.millerthomson.com/en/insights/marketing-advertising-and-product-compliance/quebec-puts-an-end-to-contest-laws/) (both accessed 2026-08-16).

## 10. Software implications

What all of the above means for FairDraw as a product. Ordered by how much legal risk each
item removes.

### A. The draw engine must be provenance-blind (highest priority)

The UK regulator's wording — *"the system that determines prizes should not recognise the
two routes as different"* — and California's *"shall not be subjected to any disability or
disadvantage in the winner selection process"* are the same requirement.

- **One pool, one weight.** Free/AMOE entries and paid entries go into the *same* array with
  the *same* per-entry weight. There must be no `entryType` field the selection function can
  see.
- Architect this as a hard boundary: the selection function takes a flat list of entry IDs.
  Provenance metadata lives in a separate structure used only for auditing and display.
- **Add a test that proves it.** A unit test that runs the draw over a mixed pool and asserts
  the observed win rate per entry is statistically indistinguishable between sources is a
  compliance artifact, not just a test.
- Weighted draws are a legitimate feature (e.g. weighted by donation tier) but must be
  **off by default** and carry an explicit warning that weighting *by payment* converts a
  sweepstakes into a lottery in most US states.

### B. Structural guardrails — make the illegal shape hard to build

- **Promotion-type wizard.** Ask three questions at setup: *Is there a prize? Is the winner
  chosen by chance? Does anyone pay or give anything of value to enter?* Three yeses →
  block, or route to "nonprofit raffle with permit" / "add an AMOE" / "make it a skill
  contest."
- **No dual-currency mechanics, ever.** Do not ship purchasable credits that yield
  redeemable entries. That is the exact model banned by CA AB 831, NY S5935A and **Maine
  LD 2007**.
- **Refuse to be the payment layer for paid entries** unless the organiser attests to
  nonprofit status + permit number, or an AMOE is configured. AB 831 extends liability to
  "platform providers" who *knowingly and willfully* support these — so **what the product
  knows matters**. Log the attestation.
- **Jurisdiction gate.** Collect organiser state and entrant-eligibility states; surface
  the state-specific warnings (Maine raffle permits; NY/FL/RI registration).

### C. AMOE as a first-class object

- Every paid-entry draw must have a linked **free entry method** with: equal prominence in
  the UI, no extra steps, no extra cost, the same entry period, and **the same per-entry
  weight**.
- Enforce **equal convenience** programmatically: if the paid path is 2 clicks, the free
  path may not be 5. Flag violations at publish time.
- Track AMOE entries separately for *reporting*, never for *selection*.

### D. Official Rules generator

Generate a rules document from the draw configuration, with all 17 elements from §5.
Because the rules must match what the software does, generate them **from the same config
object that drives the engine** — that guarantees consistency and is itself a compliance
argument.

- Include the **platform disclaimer** picker (Meta / X / TikTok / YouTube / Twitch /
  Discord) with the correct wording per platform, plus the YouTube-specific requirements
  (Community Guidelines link, privacy notice, viewer release).
- Include short-form/abbreviated rules output for captions.
- **Version and hash the rules**; store the hash in the draw receipt so it is provable which
  rules governed which draw.

### E. Receipt and audit record

The provably-fair receipt should be extended into a **compliance record** containing:
rules version hash, entry period with timezone, full entry list with timestamps and source,
disqualifications with reasons, the seed commitment and reveal, the algorithm identifier,
the winner, and the winner-notification/claim timeline.

- **Retention defaults: 4 years**, with hard floors of NY's *file winners list within 90
  days, keep records 6 months after* and FL's *hold winning entries 90 days*.
- **Immutability** — the entry list must be frozen at draw time and the freeze must be
  provable. A commit-reveal scheme where the entry-list hash is published *before* the seed
  is revealed does this.
- **Export**: a one-click "regulator packet" (rules + entry list + receipt + winners list).

### F. Winner workflow

- **Winners-list publication**: auto-generate a public winners list page (name + city +
  prize), with a configurable "request the winners list at [address/URL]" clause.
- **Affidavit / release**: trigger the affidavit + publicity-release flow automatically when
  ARV crosses a configurable threshold (default $600; notarisation prompt above ~$5,000),
  with a claim deadline countdown and automatic **alternate winner** selection on
  forfeiture — drawn from the same frozen pool with the same receipt guarantees.
- **Tax flow**: prompt for W-9 collection when a winner's aggregate prize value for the
  calendar year reaches the current threshold — **$2,000 for prizes awarded after
  31 Dec 2025** (was $600), inflation-indexed from 2027. Make this a *config value*, not a
  constant in code, because it now moves annually.
- **Canada mode**: if entrants may be Canadian, require a **skill-testing question** step
  for the selected winner before the prize is confirmed, with a time limit and one attempt.

### G. Threshold warnings engine

Watch total announced prize ARV and raise warnings at:

| Trigger | Warning |
|---|---|
| ARV > **$500** | TikTok Shop prize cap exceeded |
| ARV ≥ **$600** | Affidavit/release recommended (legacy 1099 line; some state thresholds) |
| ARV > **$500**, retail-linked | Rhode Island registration (retail establishments) |
| ARV > **$5,000** | New York (30 days ahead + bond) and Florida (7 days ahead + $100 fee + trust/bond) registration |
| ARV ≥ **$2,000** per winner/year | Form 1099-MISC and W-9 required |
| Any paid entry + chance | Lottery warning; block or require AMOE / nonprofit permit |

### H. Things the product should refuse to do

- Run a paid-entry random draw for a for-profit organiser with no AMOE.
- Give paid entries higher weight than free entries in the same draw.
- Implement "share to your timeline / tag a friend in this post for extra entries" as a
  Meta-facing entry mechanic.
- Take a cut of entry fees in a chance-based draw (that is a rake).
- Offer cash or gift cards as prizes in a TikTok Shop context.
- Operate a purchasable-credit → redeemable-sweeps-currency loop.

### I. Documentation posture

Ship a plain-language "Is my giveaway legal?" guide that reproduces the three-element test,
links to the state registration pages, and states clearly that FairDraw is a drawing tool
and **not** a provider of legal advice, with a prompt to consult a promotions attorney. The
